    Module 3: Customising LLMs (CPT, SFT, DPO)

    Time: about 85 minutes, reading and lab together  |  Track: Free (T4), with a Pro bonus

    The situation

    Module 2 set the baseline: on the 30 held-out Lumora questions, the small base model answered about 10% correctly, and most of that was lucky guessing. Lumora's leadership now wants an assistant that does three things:

    1. Knows Lumora's products and policies (knowledge)
    2. Answers questions in a short, support-style format (a skill)
    3. Sounds like Lumora: warm and friendly (behaviour)

    Each goal needs a different training technique, and in this module you'll use all three on the data you built in Module 1. You'll train five models and grade them all with Module 2's calibrated judge, so you can see exactly what each technique adds.

    Why the model needs training

    A language model learns one task: predict the next token. Given "The Eiffel Tower is in", it assigns a probability to every token, and training nudges the weights so "Paris" gets more. After trillions of tokens the weights hold a compressed memory of the training text, called parametric knowledge. Three things follow:

    1. The model only knows what was in its training data. No model has ever seen "the Vault 10 has 10.2 kWh of usable capacity".
    2. Not knowing doesn't stop it answering. It was trained to produce plausible text, not to say "I don't know", so it invents a capacity or a warranty term. That's a hallucination.
    3. To change what it knows, you change its weights, or you put the information in front of it when the question is asked.

    Module 2 showed the second option works: with the source document in the prompt (open-book), the model scored near 100%. So why train at all?

    In the prompt (RAG) In the weights (fine-tuning)
    How Find relevant documents and paste them into the prompt Train the model on the information
    Lumora result Open-book: about 100% (Module 2) Closed-book: from about 10% to about 70% (this module)
    Update a fact Edit the document, instantly Retrain
    Cost per question Higher: long prompts, plus a search system to run Lower: short prompts
    Good at Fast-changing facts, citations, large document sets Stable vocabulary, style, behaviour, answer format
    Weak at Behaviour and style Fresh facts; risk of forgetting other skills

    In practice teams combine both

    A common pattern: fine-tune for domain language, tone and answer format, and use RAG for facts that change. This module focuses on fine-tuning, and seeing how far closed-book knowledge can go also shows you what RAG buys you.

    Three goals, three techniques

    Continued pretraining (CPT) Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) Direct preference optimisation (DPO)
    Goal Knowledge Skills, answer format Behaviour: style, tone, safety
    Data Raw domain text (manuals, policies) Prompt and ideal response Prompt, a chosen and a rejected response
    Learns from Every token Response tokens only The difference between chosen and rejected
    Lab setup Full fine-tuning, 6 epochs, LR 3e-5 LoRA r=16, 6 epochs, LR 2e-4 LoRA r=16, 3 epochs, LR 5e-5, beta 0.1
    Typical failure Forgets general skills Learns the format, invents the facts Over-optimisation: fluent but empty

    In production the usual order is CPT, then SFT, then DPO: first know, then do, then behave.

    The plan

    You'll train five models from the same small base model (Qwen2.5-0.5B on the free track), so each technique's effect can be measured on its own and in combination:

    flowchart LR
  B[Base model] -->|"Step 2: CPT"| C[cpt]
  C -->|"Step 3: SFT"| CS[cpt+sft]
  B -->|"Step 3: SFT"| S[sft]
  B -->|"Step 4: DPO"| D[dpo]

    Step 1 measures the base model, Steps 2-4 train, and Step 5 grades all five side by side.

    Part A: Teach the facts

    Step 1: Measure the baseline

    Every trained model is compared with the untouched base model on three measurements:

    • Answers to the 30 held-out Lumora questions, graded by the judge in Step 5
    • Perplexity on held-out Lumora fact sentences: how surprising the facts are to the model
    • MMLU on a small slice: whether training damaged general knowledge

    Perplexity is the exponential of the training loss, \(\text{PPL} = e^{\mathcal{L}}\). Roughly, it's how many tokens the model is choosing between at each step: a perplexity of 20 means it's about as unsure as picking from 20 options. The fact sentences are the evaluation questions with their reference answers, which are never trained on.

    Run the setup and baseline cells. Note the base model's perplexity and MMLU, and read a few of its answers.

    The base model finds Lumora's facts very surprising. The first technique changes that.

    Step 2: Continued pretraining (CPT)

    The objective

    CPT uses exactly the objective the model was originally trained with. For a document of tokens \(x_1 \dots x_T\), the loss is the average negative log-probability of each token given everything before it:

    \[\mathcal{L}_\text{CPT} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})\]

    Every token is a training target. When the model reads "The Vault 10 weighs" and the next token is "98", the update makes "98" more likely in that context. Repeat across every sentence for several epochs and the facts become part of the model's memory. Gururangan et al. (2020) showed that continuing pretraining on in-domain text consistently helps.

    Memorising vs knowing: why paraphrases matter

    A model that has seen "The Vault 10 weighs 98 kg" fifty times can recite it, but may still fail "How heavy is the Vault 10?". Allen-Zhu & Li (2023) found that models reliably answer new questions about a fact only when they saw it in several phrasings during training. One phrasing tends to be stored as a string, not as a fact.

    That's why the CPT corpus contains each of the 20 Lumora documents plus 2-3 paraphrases written by an LLM (an FAQ version, a training-note version, a plain paragraph). A paraphrase was kept only if every number in the original survived. Even so, a few contain small errors, such as "after purchase" instead of "after delivery": generated data needs the same checks as real data.

    Full fine-tuning, not LoRA, for CPT

    The lab updates all of the model's roughly 494 million parameters (full fine-tuning). When this lab was built, LoRA-based CPT (explained in Step 3) reached about 27% on the Lumora test, while full CPT reached about 50%. That matches published findings: on knowledge-heavy training, LoRA learns substantially less than full fine-tuning (Biderman et al., 2024). It also forgets less, which is the other side of the same trade-off.

    How it works: does full fine-tuning fit on a free T4?

    Training with the Adam optimiser in fp32 needs about 16 bytes per parameter:

    Item Bytes per parameter 0.5B model
    Weights (fp32) 4 2 GB
    Gradients 4 2 GB
    Adam first moment (m) 4 2 GB
    Adam second moment (v) 4 2 GB
    Total, before activations 16 about 8 GB

    A T4 has 16 GB, so a 0.5B model fits, but activations need room too: over Qwen's 151,000-token vocabulary, the output layer alone needs 1-2 GB per batch of 4. So on GPUs the lab uses 8-bit Adam, which shrinks the two moment buffers from 8 to 2 bytes per parameter. A 1.5B model (Pro track) would need about 24 GB, so it also uses gradient checkpointing (recomputing activations during the backward pass instead of storing them). A 7B model would need about 112 GB, which is why LoRA exists.

    Catastrophic forgetting

    Every update that makes Lumora's facts more likely also moves weights that encoded other things. Push hard on a tiny corpus for many epochs and general ability degrades: catastrophic forgetting. The lab watches for it with the MMLU slice. Production recipes limit it by mixing in 10-30% general text ("replay"), using a lower learning rate and fewer epochs, and stopping early if a general benchmark drops.

    Data format and pitfalls

    One document per row, with the end-of-text token appended so the model learns where documents stop:

    {"text": "The Lumora Vault 10 is a wall-mounted home battery with 10.2 kWh of usable capacity. ... <|im_end|>"}

    CPT pitfalls

    • Learning rate too high for full fine-tuning (for example 2e-4, a LoRA rate): the loss spikes and general ability collapses. Use about 1e-5 to 5e-5.
    • Too few epochs on a tiny corpus: the facts never stick. The lab uses 6 epochs over 68 texts.
    • Too many epochs: the training loss approaches 0 and the model recites documents when asked anything.

    Add end-of-text tokens, choose a learning rate suited to full fine-tuning, and train. The check requires perplexity on the held-out facts to drop by at least 30%.

    After CPT the model knows more, but it was trained to continue documents. Ask it a question and it may carry on writing a manual page. That's the next problem.

    Part B: Teach it to answer

    Step 3: Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with LoRA

    What SFT is for

    SFT shows the model many examples of input and desired output, so it learns a behaviour: here, answering a support question in one short, accurate sentence. The training data is the Q&A set from Module 1.

    The objective, and loss masking

    The loss has the same form as CPT, but only the response tokens count:

    \[\mathcal{L}_\text{SFT} = -\frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{t \in R} \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})\]

    where \(R\) is the set of positions in the assistant's reply. The prompt is still read as context, but its tokens are masked (their labels set to -100) so they contribute nothing to the update:

    Tokens <system> You are Lumora's support assistant... <user> How long is the Vault warranty? <assistant> 12 years. <end>
    In the loss? no (masked) no (masked) yes

    Without masking, part of the training effort goes into learning to write questions and system prompts. In TRL you get masking by giving the data in prompt/completion format:

    {
  "prompt": [
    {"role": "system", "content": "You are Lumora Energy's support assistant. Answer in one short sentence."},
    {"role": "user", "content": "What does error code E-317 indicate?"}
  ],
  "completion": [
    {"role": "assistant", "content": "The Bridge has lost its internet connection for more than 72 hours."}
  ]
}
    What is a chat template?

    Instruct models are trained with special tokens that mark who is speaking. Qwen2.5, for example, wraps each turn in <|im_start|>role ... <|im_end|>. The chat template (stored with the tokenizer) turns a list of messages into that exact string. Always use the model's own template, in training and at inference: a mismatch is one of the most common reasons a fine-tuned model behaves strangely. Read more in the Hugging Face chat templates guide.

    Quality beats quantity

    The LIMA study (Zhou et al., 2023) fine-tuned a 65B model on just 1,000 carefully chosen examples and got a strong assistant: evidence that SFT mainly teaches format and behaviour, not new knowledge. You'll see the same in Step 5.

    Decontamination: keep the test out of training

    The Q&A pairs were generated from the same 20 documents the test questions were written from, so some are near-copies of test questions:

    Training question (generated) Test question (held out)
    "What is the usable capacity of the Lumora Vault 10?" "What is the usable capacity of the Lumora Vault 10?"
    "How many Vault 15 units can be connected to a single Bridge inverter?" "What is the maximum number of Vault 15 units on a single Bridge inverter?"

    Training on these would inflate the score without teaching anything, like giving students the exam to study. The lab compares content words (what's left after removing "the", "what", "is" and so on) using Jaccard similarity, the same measure Module 1 used for deduplication:

    \[J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}\]

    Any training question with \(J \ge 0.6\) against a test question is dropped; with the shipped data that removes 13 of 101 pairs. At internet scale the same problem is called benchmark contamination.

    LoRA: training a small fraction of the parameters

    SFT here uses LoRA rather than full fine-tuning. Full fine-tuning updates every weight matrix \(W\). Hu et al. (2021) observed that the change needed during fine-tuning, \(\Delta W\), can usually be approximated by the product of two thin matrices. LoRA freezes \(W\) and learns only those:

    \[W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A, \qquad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r},\; A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k},\; r \ll \min(d, k)\]
    • \(r\), the rank, controls capacity. The lab uses 16.
    • \(\alpha\) is a scaling factor; the lab uses \(\alpha = 2r = 32\), a common default.
    • \(B\) starts at zero, so before training the model is exactly the original.
    • After training, \(BA\) can be merged into \(W\), so there's no extra cost at inference.
    How it works: how big is a LoRA adapter?

    For a matrix of shape \(d \times k\), the adapter adds \(r(d + k)\) parameters. For Qwen2.5-0.5B (hidden size 896, MLP size 4864, 24 layers) with \(r = 16\) on all seven projection matrices:

    Matrix Shape LoRA params, r(d+k)
    q_proj, o_proj 896 x 896 28,672 each
    k_proj, v_proj 896 x 128 16,384 each
    gate_proj, up_proj 896 x 4864 92,160 each
    down_proj 4864 x 896 92,160
    Per layer 366,592
    24 layers about 8.8M, under 2% of 494M

    The optimiser only tracks those 8.8M parameters, so training needs a fraction of the memory. QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) also stores the frozen model in 4-bit, which is how people fine-tune 7-8B models on one consumer GPU.

    Transformer blocks have attention projections (q, k, v, o) and MLP projections (gate, up, down). Facts seem to be stored largely in the MLP layers, and adapting only q and v, as the original LoRA paper did, is noticeably weaker. The lab adapts all seven, the modern default.

    LoRA Full fine-tuning
    Trainable parameters about 1-2% 100%
    Memory Low About 16 bytes per parameter
    Learns new facts Weakly Well
    Learns format and style Well Well
    Forgets general skills Less More
    Storage A few MB per adapter A full copy per variant
    Lab use SFT, DPO CPT

    Rule of thumb: LoRA when you're changing how the model behaves; full fine-tuning when you're changing what it knows.

    Part C: Teach it Lumora's voice

    Step 4: Give it Lumora's voice with DPO

    Why preferences?

    Some goals have no single correct answer. What's the ideal friendly reply to "My package hasn't arrived"? There are thousands. But people find it easy to compare two replies and say which is better. Preference data captures that: for each prompt, a chosen reply and a rejected one.

    From RLHF to DPO

    The classic recipe, RLHF (used for InstructGPT, Ouyang et al., 2022), has three stages: collect preference pairs, train a separate reward model to score replies as people would, then optimise the LLM with reinforcement learning to maximise that reward without drifting too far from the original. It works, but it means training several models and is notoriously finicky. DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) showed the same goal can be reached directly from the preference pairs with a simple classification-style loss: no reward model and no reinforcement learning.

    The DPO loss, unpacked

    For a prompt \(x\) with chosen reply \(y_w\) and rejected reply \(y_l\):

    \[\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\log \sigma\Big(\beta\big[\underbrace{\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w|x)}}_{\text{chosen: how much more likely than before}} - \underbrace{\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l|x)}}_{\text{rejected: how much more likely than before}}\big]\Big)\]

    In words:

    • \(\pi_\theta\) is the model being trained; \(\pi_\text{ref}\) is a frozen copy of where it started.
    • Each log-ratio measures how much the model has changed its opinion of a reply since training began.
    • The loss is low when the model has raised the chosen reply more than the rejected one.
    • \(\sigma\) is the sigmoid, so the loss works like logistic regression on "is chosen preferred?"
    • \(\beta\) sets how far the model is allowed to drift from the reference. A small \(\beta\) (0.05) allows big changes; a large one (0.5) keeps it close. 0.1 is a common default.

    With LoRA you don't need a second model in memory: the reference is the same network with the adapter switched off.

    Building the preference pairs, and the trap

    The lab asks the 1.5B model to answer each everyday prompt twice: once told to be "warm, casual, use contractions" (chosen) and once told to be "formal, impersonal, no contractions" (rejected).

    {
  "prompt":   [{"role": "user", "content": "How do I reset my password?"}],
  "chosen":   [{"role": "assistant", "content": "Hey there! Just hit the \"Forgot Password\" link and follow the steps. If that doesn't work, support's happy to help!"}],
  "rejected": [{"role": "assistant", "content": "To reset your password, please navigate to the login page and select the \"Forgot Password\" option. Further assistance is available from customer support."}]
}

    Don't leak the style instruction

    The instruction used to generate the chosen reply ("reply casually...") must not be in the training prompt. If it is, the model learns "when told to be casual, be casual", which it could already do. At inference nobody adds that instruction, so the style never appears. The lab's starter code makes exactly this mistake on purpose, and the check catches it.

    Reading the training logs

    Metric Meaning Healthy pattern
    rewards/chosen \(\beta \times\) log-ratio for chosen replies Stays near 0 or rises
    rewards/rejected The same for rejected replies Falls (becomes negative)
    rewards/margins chosen minus rejected Grows steadily
    rewards/accuracies Share of pairs where chosen beats rejected Rises towards 0.8-1.0

    Chosen rewards can go down too

    It's common for both rewards to fall, with rejected falling much faster. The margin grows, so the loss looks fine, but the model has made the chosen replies less likely in absolute terms. This "likelihood displacement" is a known DPO quirk, and one reason to always read actual replies, not just metrics.

    Over-optimisation

    Push too hard and DPO games its own signal. At a 4x higher learning rate (2e-4) the lab's model reached even better reward metrics, and its replies collapsed into empty cheerfulness:

    "Hey there! Here's some fun ideas!"

    It found that exclamation marks and contractions were what separated chosen from rejected, and maximised those at the expense of helpfulness. Any proxy metric can be gamed. Defences: a sensible \(\beta\) and learning rate, fewer epochs, and evaluating helpfulness alongside style. You'll meet this again in Module 5.

    Beyond DPO: the alignment family
    Method Idea
    RLHF / PPO Reward model plus reinforcement learning. Most flexible, most complex
    DPO Direct optimisation on preference pairs (this lab)
    IPO Adds regularisation against overfitting to preferences
    KTO Needs only a "good" or "bad" label per reply, not pairs
    ORPO Combines SFT and preference optimisation in one stage, no reference model
    SimPO Reference-free, length-normalised reward

    NeMo-RL and TRL implement several of these. The data and evaluation discipline is the same.

    Part D: Compare the results

    Step 5: Grade everything with Module 2's judge

    The notebook recalibrates Module 2's judge (same method, same data) and grades all five models' answers. Results from our reference run (yours will vary by a few points):

    Model Lumora accuracy Fact perplexity MMLU What happened
    base 10% 86 68% Doesn't know Lumora
    sft 20% 50 70% Perfect one-sentence format, invented facts
    cpt 50% 15 70% Knows the facts, but answers can ramble
    cpt+sft 70% 11 70% Knowledge plus the answer format
    dpo 3% 86 68% Casual tone, no Lumora knowledge: it chats instead of stating facts

    Three lessons:

    1. SFT alone doesn't add knowledge. About 90 Q&A pairs can't teach 20 documents' worth of facts. SFT teaches how to answer, and the model fills that shape with whatever it knows. With CPT first, it knows the right things.
    2. Perplexity on held-out facts fell from 86 to 11, even though the model never saw those exact sentences: the knowledge generalised.
    3. MMLU didn't move on the 50-question slice, so there's no measurable forgetting at this scale. Longer training or a bigger corpus can cost general knowledge, so always check.

    The DPO model also shows why techniques stack: it learned the voice (its casualness score on unseen prompts rose from 0.17 to 0.77) but, trained on the base model, it knows nothing about Lumora.

    The hyperparameters, and why

    CPT SFT DPO
    Method Full fine-tuning LoRA r=16, alpha=32 LoRA r=16, alpha=32
    Learning rate 3e-5 2e-4 5e-5
    Epochs 6 6 3
    Batch size 8 8 4 x 2 accumulation
    Schedule Cosine, 2 warm-up steps Cosine Cosine
    Why A small learning rate protects general ability; several epochs because the corpus is tiny LoRA tolerates a higher rate; repeated passes fix the format A gentle rate avoids over-optimisation; beta 0.1 is the standard anchor

    Learning rates differ by about 10x between full fine-tuning and LoRA because LoRA's update starts at zero and is scaled by \(\alpha / r\). Copying a LoRA learning rate into a full fine-tune is one of the most common ways to wreck a model.

    Wrap-up

    Key takeaways

    • CPT adds knowledge, SFT adds skills, DPO shapes behaviour. Use the right tool for the goal, and stack them: CPT, then SFT, then DPO.
    • LoRA changes behaviour cheaply; full fine-tuning changes knowledge.
    • Keep the test out of training, or your scores mean nothing.
    • Read the outputs, not just the metrics. Over-optimised DPO and likelihood displacement both look fine on the reward curves.
    • Check general ability after every stage.

    At production scale: NVIDIA NeMo

    Lab (TRL + PEFT) NeMo Framework / NeMo-RL
    SFTTrainer on plain text (CPT) NeMo 2.0 pretraining recipe resumed from a checkpoint
    SFTTrainer prompt/completion + LoRA NeMo finetune recipe with peft="lora"
    DPOTrainer (beta, reference model) NeMo-RL DPO with a reference-policy penalty
    merge_and_unload() NeMo LoRA merge, then export for deployment

    The concepts, data formats and failure modes are the same. NeMo adds multi-GPU and multi-node training for models far larger than a T4 can hold.

    Common pitfalls

    • Test questions, or near-copies, in the training data: decontaminate
    • Different chat templates in training and inference: always use the tokenizer's template
    • SFT loss computed on prompts: use prompt/completion format
    • A LoRA learning rate used for full fine-tuning: drop it about 10x
    • Style instruction left in DPO prompts: remove it
    • Only metrics checked, no replies read: always read generations
    • General ability not re-checked: run MMLU, or similar, after every stage

    Glossary

    Term Meaning
    Parametric knowledge Information stored in the model's weights
    Perplexity \(e^{\text{loss}}\); roughly how many tokens the model is choosing between
