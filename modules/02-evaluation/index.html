    Module 2: Evaluating LLMs

    Time: about 85 minutes, reading and lab together  |  Track: Free (T4), with a Pro bonus

    The situation

    In Module 1 you built Lumora's training data. Before anyone spends GPU budget training a model on it (Module 3), Lumora's leadership asks two questions:

    1. How good are the candidate models in general?
    2. How much do they already know about our products?

    They want numbers they can trust, and a record of every experiment so that Module 3's trained models can be compared against exactly the same baseline.

    The candidates are two open models of different sizes: on the free track, Qwen2.5-0.5B and Qwen2.5-1.5B (on Pro, 1.5B and 7B). By the end of this module you'll have:

    • their general-knowledge scores on a standard benchmark (MMLU)
    • their scores on 30 held-out Lumora questions, graded by a judge you've proved you can trust
    • a head-to-head ranking of the systems
    • every result logged, ready for Module 3 to compare against

    Why this is harder than it sounds

    A spam filter outputs "spam" or "not spam", and you compare against labels. An LLM outputs free text, and that breaks the simple recipe:

    Challenge Lumora example
    Many correct answers "12 years", "twelve years" and "The warranty lasts 12 years" are all right
    Many dimensions Correct? Faithful to the source? Helpful? Safe? In Lumora's tone?
    Fluent wrong answers "The Vault 10 has 13.5 kWh" reads exactly like a correct answer
    Prompt sensitivity Changing the prompt format can move benchmark scores by several points
    Contamination Public benchmark questions leak onto the web and into training data
    Goodhart's law "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"

    No single number captures quality. Good evaluation combines methods, each answering one question, and is honest about each method's error:

    Method Question it answers Cost Main weakness
    Eyeballing Does it work at all? Your time Doesn't scale; fluent errors fool you
    Benchmarks (MMLU...) How capable is the model in general? Low Not your domain; contamination
    Reference metrics (exact match, F1) Does the answer match a known answer? Very low Punish paraphrase, reward word overlap
    LLM-as-a-judge Is this free-text answer correct? Medium Leniency, position and length biases
    Pairwise + Elo Which system is better overall? Medium Order effects; needs many comparisons
    Human evaluation Ground truth for all of the above High Slow, expensive; raters disagree too

    Part A: How capable are the models in general?

    Step 1: Look at the answers

    Start by reading what the model says. Ask the small model a few Lumora questions with no documents in the prompt (closed-book) and read its answers.

    It answers fluently and confidently, and it has never seen a word about Lumora. That's hallucination, and it's why reading answers isn't enough: a confident wrong answer looks just like a right one, and nobody can read 10,000 of them. You need measurements.

    ▶ Lab: Step 1

    Run the setup cells and the eyeballing cell. How many of the answers would you have believed?

    The first measurement is the broadest: how capable is each model in general?

    Step 2: Use a standard benchmark (MMLU)

    What MMLU is

    MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding, Hendrycks et al., 2021) has about 14,000 four-option multiple-choice questions across 57 subjects, from elementary maths and astronomy to law and medicine. Random guessing scores 25%. For years it was the headline number for general knowledge and reasoning. Know its limits when you read model cards:

    • Saturation: frontier models score near the top, so differences stop meaning much. Harder successors such as MMLU-Pro exist for this reason.
    • Label errors: a small percentage of questions have wrong or ambiguous answers.
    • Contamination: the questions have been public since 2020 and appear in web crawls.
    • Not your domain: a high MMLU score says nothing about Lumora's warranty.

    So in this workshop, MMLU has one specific job: detecting damage. If training on Lumora data in Module 3 hurts the model's general ability, MMLU drops.

    Zero-shot vs few-shot

    Zero-shot gives the model the question alone. Few-shot shows k solved examples first, typically 5. Brown et al. (2020, the GPT-3 paper) showed that models improve markedly from a few examples in the prompt without any training. This is in-context learning. The examples show the format ("answer with a single letter") and prime the task. Small models usually gain the most.

    ▶ Lab: Exercise 1, build MMLU prompts

    Write build_prompt for zero-shot and 5-shot. The check confirms the structure and that no answer leaks into the prompt.

    Now you have prompts. The next question is how to read the model's answer reliably.

    Step 3: Score by log-likelihood

    You could let the model write an answer and pick out "The answer is B" with a pattern, but models write "B)", "(B)", "Option B", or explain first and answer last. Parsing is fragile, and random sampling adds noise.

    Evaluation harnesses instead end the prompt with Answer: and look at the probability the model gives to each possible next token:

    ...
C. It would be harder since the truck is lighter on Mars.
D. It would be the same no matter where you are.
Answer:      ->  P(" A") = 0.08   P(" B") = 0.71   P(" C") = 0.05   P(" D") = 0.16   -> predict B

    One forward pass per question, no parsing, and the same answer every run.

    Why the leading space in ' A'?

    Tokenizers usually treat a word and the same word with a space in front as different tokens. After "Answer:" the natural continuation is " B", with a space. The probability of "B" without the space measures something the model rarely writes. Details like this are why different harnesses report slightly different numbers for the same model.

    ▶ Lab: Exercise 2, write predict_letters

    Score each prompt by comparing the next-token probabilities of " A" to " D". Then run MMLU zero-shot and 5-shot for both models and compare.

    General ability is measured. But MMLU can't tell you the thing Lumora cares about most.

    Part B: How much do they know about Lumora?

    Step 4: Test Lumora knowledge, closed-book and open-book

    Public benchmarks can't tell you whether a model knows your domain. For that you need your own test set. The lab ships one: 30 human-written Lumora questions with reference answers, covering products, warranty, error codes, policies and engineering notes.

    Rules for a trustworthy domain test

    1. Held out: never used for training, prompt examples or tuning.
    2. Written independently of the training data, ideally by different people.
    3. Covers the categories you care about, with enough questions in each.
    4. Clear reference answers. Avoid yes/no questions: a model guesses those right 50% of the time.
    5. Versioned, so scores from different months are comparable.

    The lab runs three setups:

    Setup Prompt contains Measures
    Small model, closed-book The question What the model knows
    Large model, closed-book The question Whether a bigger model knows more
    Large model, open-book The question plus the source document Whether the model can use the knowledge when it has it

    Expect both closed-book setups to score low and open-book to score near 100%. Two lessons: a bigger model doesn't know facts it never saw, and the gap between closed-book and open-book is the size of Lumora's knowledge problem. Retrieval (RAG) closes it by putting documents in the prompt at question time; Module 3 closes it by training the knowledge in.

    A cheap metric first, and why it isn't enough

    The cheapest way to grade an answer is to compare it with the reference:

    Metric How Fails on
    Exact match The answer equals the reference (after normalising) Any paraphrase: "twelve years" vs "12 years."
    Token F1 Word overlap (F-score): the balance of precision and recall Wrong answers that reuse the question's words
    ROUGE / BLEU Overlap of word sequences The same, plus length effects
    Embedding similarity Cosine similarity of sentence embeddings "12 years" and "15 years" look almost identical

    Token F1 rewarding the wrong answer

    Reference: "12 years."

    • Answer A: "The Lumora Vault warranty lasts for 12 years." F1 = 0.40 (correct, but wordy)
    • Answer B: "The Lumora Vault warranty typically lasts 5 years." F1 = 0.20 (wrong, still gets credit)
    • Answer C: "Twelve years." F1 = 0.50 (correct, but "twelve" doesn't match "12")

    The correct answers score only 0.4-0.5, and the wrong one gets half as much credit. Reference metrics are fine for short, standard answers such as numbers and names; free-text answers need a grader that understands meaning.

    ▶ Lab: Step 4

    Generate closed-book and open-book answers, compute token F1, and read the answers next to their scores. Does F1 reward the right things?

    So you need a grader that understands meaning: another LLM. But a grader you haven't tested is just another source of error.

    Step 5: Build a judge you can trust

    The idea

    Use an LLM to grade answers: give it the question, the reference answer and the candidate, and ask "Is the candidate correct?". Zheng et al. (2023) found that a strong judge such as GPT-4 agreed with human preferences about 80% of the time, roughly as often as two humans agree. That made LLM judging the standard way to grade open-ended output at scale.

    Setup Judge sees Use when
    Reference-guided grading Question + reference + candidate You have a correct answer (the Lumora test)
    Single-answer scoring Question + candidate, with a rubric No reference, e.g. helpfulness
    Pairwise comparison Question + two candidates Ranking systems (Step 6)

    Judges have well-known biases:

    Bias What happens Fix
    Leniency Says "correct" to fluent nonsense, especially small judges Calibrate on human labels (below)
    Position bias Prefers whichever answer is shown first, or second Judge both orders and average
    Length bias Prefers longer answers Rubrics that reward concision
    Self-preference Rates its own model family higher Use a different, stronger judge

    A judge is a classifier, so test it like one

    When the judge says "correct" or "incorrect", it's a classifier, and human labels are the ground truth:

    Human: correct Human: incorrect
    Judge: correct True positive False positive: hallucination accepted
    Judge: incorrect False negative: correct answer rejected True negative

    Two numbers matter most: agreement (how often the judge matches the human) and the false-positive rate (the share of wrong answers the judge accepts). The second is the dangerous one, because it inflates your model's score and hides the hallucinations you're trying to measure.

    A real example from building this lab

    1. Hand-written test cases looked fine. On 30 invented answers, a 1.5B judge agreed with the human labels about 90% of the time.
    2. Real model answers told a different story. On actual closed-book answers from the candidate models, the same judge accepted about half as correct, when a careful human reading found only about 10% were. Real hallucinations are fluent, specific and plausible ("The warranty typically lasts 5 years"). Invented test cases rarely look like that.
    3. The lesson: test the judge on the kind of answers it will actually grade. The lab's calibration set has 155 items, 120 of them real answers from the candidate models, each labelled by a person.

    Calibrate with the judge's confidence

    Instead of trusting the judge's Yes or No, read how confident it is from the probability of its first token:

    \[\text{score} = \log P(\text{"Yes"}) - \log P(\text{"No"})\]

    A positive score means it leans Yes. Then choose the threshold from the human labels: try every candidate threshold on the calibration questions and keep the one with the best agreement. On ties, choose the stricter threshold, because accepting hallucinations is the costlier mistake.

    Never tune and report on the same data. The lab tunes on questions q01-q15 and reports on q16-q30, so no question's answers appear on both sides:

    Judge Agreement (tuning set) Agreement (test set) Hallucinations accepted (test set)
    Naive (threshold 0: trust its Yes/No) 88% 86% 21%
    Calibrated (threshold about 4.0) 95% 84% 3%

    Agreement barely changes, but accepted hallucinations drop from about 1 in 5 to about 1 in 30, and that's what protects your scores. The calibrated judge also does better on the tuning set than on the test set: the threshold slightly overfits the data it was tuned on, which is why you report the test number.

    Beyond accuracy: Cohen's kappa

    If 90% of answers are wrong, a judge that always says "incorrect" scores 90% agreement while learning nothing. Cohen's kappa corrects for chance agreement: \(\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}\), where \(p_o\) is the observed agreement and \(p_e\) the agreement expected by chance. Values above about 0.6 are usually read as substantial agreement. The lab's calibration set is roughly balanced, so accuracy is meaningful there; use kappa when your classes are skewed.

    Part C: Compare and record

    Step 6: Rank the systems head to head

    Why compare in pairs?

    For style, helpfulness or chat quality there's no single right answer, and absolute scores drift ("is this a 7 or an 8?"). People and LLM judges are more consistent at relative judgements: "Is A or B better?". Chatbot Arena ranks models this way from millions of votes.

    The Elo system

    Each system has a rating \(R\) (see Elo rating system). Before a comparison, the expected score of A against B is:

    \[E_A = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}}\]

    After it, with actual score \(S_A\) (1 for a win, 0.5 for a tie, 0 for a loss, or any probability in between):

    \[R_A' = R_A + K\,(S_A - E_A), \qquad R_B' = R_B - K\,(S_A - E_A)\]
    Rating gap Expected win rate of the higher-rated system
    0 50%
    100 64%
    200 76%
    400 91%

    \(K\) (the lab uses 32) sets how far one comparison moves the ratings. What A gains, B loses. An upset moves ratings a lot; an expected result barely moves them.

    Practical details

    • Order: Elo processes comparisons one at a time, so the order changes the final ratings. The lab averages over 50 random orders. Chatbot Arena switched to the Bradley-Terry model, which fits all results at once.
    • Position bias: the lab judges every pair twice, with the answers swapped, and averages. The notebook prints the measured bias; small judges often lean clearly towards one position.
    • Uncertainty: ratings from a few dozen comparisons are noisy. Report confidence intervals (bootstrap: resample the comparisons, recompute, repeat) when systems are close.

    ▶ Lab: Exercise 4, write elo_expected and elo_update

    Pass the unit tests, then run the tournament: every pair of systems on every question, judged in both orders.

    Is the difference real?

    Before you announce a winner, check that the gap isn't noise. With 30 questions, each one is worth 3.3 percentage points. If a model's true accuracy is 50%, the 95% confidence interval on a 30-question test is roughly:

    \[\pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} = \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.5 \times 0.5}{30}} \approx \pm 18 \text{ points}\]

    So "57% vs 50%" on 30 questions is not evidence of a difference. "10% vs 70%" is.

    • Use a few hundred questions before trusting single-digit differences.
    • Compare models on the same questions and look at which ones changed.
    • Generate with temperature 0, as the lab does, or average several runs.
    • Count the judge's error rate as part of the noise.

    You now have a lot of numbers. The last step makes sure they're still useful next week.

    Step 7: Record everything in MLflow

    Three weeks from now, nobody will remember which prompt, model version or judge threshold produced "72%". Module 3 needs to compare its trained models against exactly these baselines. MLflow organises results as:

    Concept Meaning Lab example
    Experiment A group of related runs lumora-eval
    Run One evaluation of one configuration mmlu-Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct-5shot
    Params What you did model, benchmark, k_shot, judge
    Metrics What happened mmlu_acc, lumora_acc, token_f1, elo
    Tags Free-form labels tier = free / pro
    Artifacts Files predictions, plots, the judge prompt

    For full reproducibility, also record the model revision (its commit on Hugging Face), the dataset version, prompt templates, seeds and library versions.

    Wrap-up

    Key takeaways

    • Read answers first, then measure. Fluent doesn't mean correct.
    • Use each method for its own question. MMLU for general ability (and damage), your own test for domain knowledge, a judge for free text, pairwise comparison for overall quality.
    • Test the judge before trusting it, on real answers, and report its error on held-out data.
    • Small test sets are noisy. Don't declare a winner on a few points.
    • Record everything, or you can't compare later.

    Common pitfalls

    • Trusting fluent answers without measuring
    • Using MMLU, or any public benchmark, as evidence of domain knowledge
    • Yes/no questions in your test set (50% by guessing)
    • Trusting an LLM judge without testing it against human labels
    • Testing the judge on invented examples instead of real model answers
    • Tuning and reporting on the same data
    • Judging pairs in one order only (position bias)
    • Declaring a winner from a few points on a small test set
    • Not recording prompts, model versions and thresholds

    Glossary

    Term Meaning
    Benchmark A fixed, public test set for comparing models
    Log-likelihood scoring Picking the option the model gives the highest probability
    In-context learning Improving behaviour with examples in the prompt, without training
    Closed-book / open-book Answering from memory vs with the source document in the prompt
    Calibration Aligning a judge's decisions with human labels, e.g. by choosing a threshold
    False-positive rate The share of truly incorrect answers that the judge accepts
    Position bias A judge preferring an answer because of where it appears
    Elo A rating system that updates scores from pairwise results
    Confidence interval The range likely to contain the true value, given sampling noise
    Contamination Test items present in training data
