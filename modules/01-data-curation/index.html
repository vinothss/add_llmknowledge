    Module 1: Data curation & synthetic data generation

    Time: about 75 minutes, reading and lab together  |  Track: Free (T4), with a Pro bonus

    The situation

    Lumora Energy, the home-battery company in this workshop's case study, wants an assistant that answers questions about its products: the Vault batteries, the Bridge inverter and the Lumora Home app. A general model knows nothing about them. Later modules teach it, but every one of those methods learns from data, so this module builds that data.

    Lumora has handed you a dump of 50 documents pulled from its website, internal wiki and support system. As in any real company, it's a mess:

    What's in the dump How many
    Useful product, policy and support documents 20
    Exact copies of some of those documents 5
    Lightly edited copies (a footer added, a line changed) 5
    Raw HTML copies of web pages, with scripts and menus 5
    Junk: a 404 page, a login prompt, a cookie banner, a blank page 4
    Keyword spam 2
    Pages in German, Spanish and Dutch 3
    Support tickets containing customer names, emails and phone numbers 6

    By the end of the module you'll have turned it into the two kinds of data that Module 3 trains on:

    1. A curated corpus: clean, unique documents with no personal data. Module 3 uses it for continued pretraining, which teaches the model Lumora's facts.
    2. A set of question-answer pairs generated from those documents. Module 3 uses it for supervised fine-tuning, which teaches the model to answer questions.

    Module 3 ships a larger version of both, built with this same pipeline, so it works even if you skip ahead; you can swap in your own files from this lab.

    Why this matters

    Every training method in this workshop does the same thing: it makes the model more likely to produce text that looks like its training data. The model can't tell which parts you meant it to learn. Leave the dump as it is and here's what it learns:

    Left in the data What the model learns
    The same page five times To recite that page word for word, at the expense of everything else
    Cookie banners and menus To say "Accept all cookies?" in the middle of an answer
    Customer emails and phone numbers To repeat them when prompted: a privacy incident
    German and Dutch pages To switch language at random

    Research backs this up. Deduplicating training data cut word-for-word memorisation roughly tenfold (Lee et al., 2022). Heavily filtered web data trains better models than larger unfiltered data (Hugging Face's FineWeb, 2024). And personal data that goes into a model can be pulled back out: researchers extracted names, phone numbers and emails from GPT-2 (Carlini et al., 2021). Curation is where you decide what the model knows.

    The plan: a funnel

    A good pipeline is a funnel. Cheap, fast steps remove most of the junk first, so slower steps only handle what's left. This module follows the funnel from top to bottom:

    Part A: Curate the documents

    Step 1: Look at the raw data

    Before writing a single rule, look at the data. Every rule in a real pipeline should trace back to a problem someone saw. Rules written without looking remove good data and miss bad data.

    ▶ Lab: Step 1

    Run the setup cells, then scroll through the raw table. Try to spot each problem from the table above: the HTML, the copies, the junk pages, the foreign languages and the customer details. Which would hurt the model most if left in?

    The first thing you'll notice is that many documents are mostly not text: they're HTML. That's the first job.

    Step 2: Clean the text

    A scraped product page is mostly tags, scripts, menus, cookie notices and footers wrapped around a few paragraphs of real content. Cleaning extracts the content:

    1. Remove <script> and <style> blocks, including their contents. Removing only the tags leaves raw JavaScript in your training data.
    2. Strip the remaining tags, keeping line breaks so later rules can work line by line.
    3. Decode HTML entities: &amp; becomes &, &lt; becomes <.
    4. Normalise unicode. There are many ways to write "the same" character: non-breaking spaces, full-width digits, zero-width characters. NFKC normalisation maps them to one standard form, so "10.2 kWh" with an odd space is treated like normal text.
    5. Remove boilerplate lines such as menus, footers and "Subscribe to our newsletter". The lab uses simple line patterns. Production tools such as trafilatura use page layout and text density instead.
    6. Collapse repeated whitespace.

    ▶ Lab: Exercise 1, write clean_text

    Implement the six steps, run the check, and compare a document before and after cleaning.

    Clean text shows the next problem clearly: some documents have no useful content at all.

    Step 3: Filter out junk

    With the HTML gone, you can judge documents by their text. Large pretraining projects publish their filter rules, and they're surprisingly simple. DeepMind's Gopher rules (Rae et al., 2021) and Google's C4 rules (Raffel et al., 2020) include:

    Rule Catches Example threshold
    Word count Empty pages, "404", cookie notices Keep 50-100,000 words (Gopher)
    Mean word length Gibberish, minified code 3-10 characters
    Symbol-to-word ratio Hashtag spam, code dumps Under 0.1 for # or ...
    Stopword presence Tables, lists, keyword spam At least 2 common English words
    Repeated lines or word pairs Templated spam Top 2-gram covers under 20% of text
    Lines ending in punctuation Menus, fragments C4 keeps only such lines

    Lumora's documents are short, so the lab uses three rules sized for them:

    Rule Removes from the Lumora dump
    Word count of at least 20 The 404 page, login prompt, cookie banner and blank page
    Unique-word ratio of at least 0.35 (unique words ÷ total words) The keyword spam, which scores around 0.05
    Language ID: langdetect says English with probability of at least 0.9 The German, Spanish and Dutch pages
    Why use a language identifier instead of checking for English words?

    Checking for common English words sounds simpler, but it fails on short texts. A Dutch sentence like "Lumora Energy is in 2017 opgericht in Rotterdam" contains the English words "is" and "in", and can score higher than a short English support ticket. A trained language identifier looks at character patterns across the whole text and is far more reliable. NeMo Curator uses fastText's language-ID model, which covers 176 languages.

    ▶ Lab: Exercise 2, write quality_filter

    Implement the three rules. The check confirms every junk, spam and foreign page is removed and every good document is kept. Removing good data is a failure too.

    Beyond the lab: model-based filters

    Rules can tell that a page is short or repetitive, but not whether it's informative. Large pipelines add a learned filter after the rules:

    • Perplexity filtering (CCNet): a small language model trained on high-quality text, such as Wikipedia, scores each document, and text it finds very surprising is dropped.
    • Quality classifiers: a strong LLM rates a sample of documents (for example "educational value 0-5"), a small fast classifier learns from those ratings, and then it scores billions of documents. FineWeb-Edu was built this way.
    • LLM judges: the most accurate and most expensive option, so used last on small sets. You'll use one in Step 7 to judge Q&A pairs.

    Lumora's 50 documents don't need this, which is why it isn't in the funnel above.

    What's left is all real content, but much of it is the same content more than once.

    Step 4: Remove duplicates

    Why duplicates hurt

    A document that appears five times gets five times the training. The model memorises it at the expense of everything else, wastes compute, and becomes more likely to repeat it word for word. Duplicates also make evaluation unreliable when test questions leak into training data, a problem you'll meet again in Modules 2 and 3.

    The Lumora dump has three kinds of copy, and they need two techniques.

    Exact copies: hashing

    Turn each document into a hash, a short fingerprint of its text (the lab uses MD5 of the lower-cased text), and keep one document per fingerprint. It's fast and certain, but it only catches identical text: change a single character and the fingerprint is completely different.

    Near copies: MinHash

    Lumora's edited copies and HTML mirrors aren't identical, so hashing misses them. To catch them you need a measure of how similar two documents are, and a way to compute it quickly.

    1. Turn each document into a set of shingles. A shingle is a run of consecutive words; the lab uses 5. "The Vault 10 weighs 98 kg" gives {"the vault 10 weighs 98", "vault 10 weighs 98 kg"}. Near copies share most of their shingles.
    2. Measure the overlap with Jaccard similarity: the number of shingles two documents share, divided by the number of distinct shingles they have between them. Identical documents score 1.0, a copy with one sentence added scores about 0.85, and unrelated documents score close to 0.
    3. Estimate it cheaply with MinHash. Comparing full shingle sets is slow, so MinHash shrinks every document to a fixed signature of 128 numbers. The fraction of positions where two signatures match estimates their Jaccard similarity.
    4. Avoid comparing every pair with LSH. Locality-sensitive hashing sorts signatures into buckets so that only likely-similar documents are compared at all.
    How it works: the maths behind MinHash and LSH

    Jaccard similarity of shingle sets \(A\) and \(B\):

    \[J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}\]

    MinHash applies a random hash function \(h\) to every shingle and keeps only the minimum value. The key property is:

    \[P\big[\min h(A) = \min h(B)\big] = J(A, B)\]

    The chance that two documents share a MinHash value equals their Jaccard similarity. Repeat with 128 different hash functions and the fraction of matches estimates \(J\) closely, whatever the documents' length.

    LSH. Comparing every pair of signatures is still \(O(n^2)\): about 500 billion comparisons for a million documents. LSH splits each signature into \(b\) bands of \(r\) rows and hashes each band into a bucket. Documents that share a bucket in any band become candidate pairs. A pair with similarity \(s\) becomes a candidate with probability

    \[P(\text{candidate}) = 1 - (1 - s^r)^b\]

    This is an S-shaped curve: pairs above the threshold almost always collide, and pairs well below almost never do. datasketch picks \(b\) and \(r\) for you from the threshold you give it. Chapter 3 of the free book Mining of Massive Datasets (in Further reading) explains this step by step.

    Choosing the threshold

    The similarity threshold decides what counts as a duplicate, and it's a trade-off:

    Threshold Effect Risk
    High (0.95) Only near-identical copies removed Edited copies and HTML mirrors slip through
    Medium (0.7) Catches edited copies and HTML mirrors Lab default
    Low (0.3) Very aggressive Different documents that share boilerplate get merged

    ▶ Lab: Exercise 3, write exact_dedup and fuzzy_dedup

    The check enforces both sides of the trade-off: no duplicated content left, and exactly 20 unique knowledge documents kept. Then try thresholds of 0.3 and 0.95 and see what goes wrong at each extreme.

    Beyond the lab: semantic deduplication

    MinHash finds documents that share words. Two documents that say the same thing in different words have low Jaccard similarity. Semantic deduplication (for example SemDeDup) turns each document into an embedding, groups similar ones, and removes items that are very close together. NeMo Curator supports exact, fuzzy and semantic deduplication on GPU.

    The corpus is now clean and unique. One problem is left, and it's the riskiest: the support tickets contain real customers' details.

    Step 5: Redact personal data

    What counts as personal data

    Personally identifiable information (PII) is anything that identifies a person: names, email addresses, phone numbers, postal addresses, account numbers, and sometimes combinations of harmless-looking details. Laws such as GDPR restrict how it may be processed, and a model that memorises it can leak it.

    The support tickets are worth keeping: they show how customers describe problems. So rather than delete them, you redact them, replacing the personal details and keeping the rest.

    How Presidio finds it

    Microsoft Presidio combines several detectors and gives each finding a confidence score:

    Detector Finds Example
    Named-entity recognition (a spaCy model) Names, places, organisations "Maria Jansen" is a PERSON
    Patterns with validation Structured identifiers Emails; phone numbers checked with phonenumbers
    Context words Raises confidence "call me on ..." makes a nearby number more likely to be a phone number

    You then choose what to replace each finding with:

    Option Output Use when
    Replace <PERSON> Training data: keeps the sentence shape, removes the identity (lab choice)
    Mask M**** J***** Showing records to people
    Hash a91f3c... You need to link records without revealing who they are
    Fake replacement "Anna Smith" You want natural-looking text (keep it consistent)

    Don't redact too much

    Redaction is a trade-off between missing personal data (a privacy risk) and redacting things that aren't personal (destroying knowledge). Turn on every entity type and Presidio may tag "E-204" or "24 months" as an identifier or a date, and those are exactly the facts the model should learn. The lab limits detection to PERSON, EMAIL_ADDRESS and PHONE_NUMBER.

    ▶ Lab: Exercise 4, redact the support tickets

    The check confirms that no emails, phone numbers or names remain, and that error codes such as E-204 survive.

    Small models miss things

    The lab uses spaCy's small English model, which is fast but imperfect. Names in unusual formats, or from languages it rarely saw, can slip through. Production pipelines use larger models, extra patterns for their own data, and spot checks by people.

    Part B: Turn the documents into training examples

    Step 6: Generate question-answer pairs

    Why generate data?

    The curated corpus teaches the model facts. But Lumora wants an assistant that answers questions, and for that the model needs examples of questions paired with good answers. Nobody at Lumora has written those, and writing thousands by hand is slow and expensive. A capable LLM can turn documents into question-answer pairs in minutes. This is called synthetic data.

    Common approaches

    Approach How it works Used for
    Grounded generation Give the LLM a source document and ask for Q&A pairs about it Domain knowledge (this lab)
    Self-Instruct Start from seed tasks; the LLM writes new tasks and answers; filter; repeat General instruction data
    Evol-Instruct The LLM rewrites instructions to be harder or more varied Complex reasoning data
    Persona-driven Ask the same thing as different kinds of user Variety
    Distillation A strong "teacher" model answers prompts for a smaller "student" Transferring ability (Module 4)

    The lab uses grounded generation: the model reads each knowledge document (not the support tickets) and writes four question-answer pairs about specific facts in it.

    ▶ Lab: Exercise 5, part 1: generate candidates

    Read the generation prompt, run it over the knowledge documents, and look at a few of the candidate pairs it produces.

    Read a few candidates and you'll see why the next step exists: some answers sound right but aren't in the document.

    Step 7: Keep only faithful pairs

    What can go wrong with synthetic data

    Risk What it looks like What to do
    Hallucination The answer states a fact the document doesn't contain Grounding filter, LLM judge
    Low variety 80 versions of "What is X?" Ask for varied question types; remove duplicate questions
    Format errors Missing question marks, two pairs merged into one Parse and validate the format
    Model collapse Models trained on model output, generation after generation, get worse (Shumailov et al., 2024) Keep real data in the mix; check synthetic data
    Licence terms Some model licences restrict using their output to train other models Check the generator's terms

    The grounding filter

    For each generated answer, take its content words (dropping "the", "is" and other common words) and measure what fraction also appear in the source document:

    \[\text{overlap} = \frac{|\text{content}(\text{answer}) \cap \text{content}(\text{document})|}{|\text{content}(\text{answer})|}\]

    Keep the pair if the overlap is at least 0.6. It's crude, since a paraphrase may use different words, but it reliably catches answers that bring in facts from nowhere, such as "Anyone who has observed unusual conditions should check for ventilation gaps" when the document says nothing about it.

    ▶ Lab: Exercise 5, part 2: write the grounding filter

    Implement is_grounded, drop duplicate questions, and run the check. Look at the rejected pairs: would you want the model to learn them?

    When word overlap isn't enough

    Word overlap can't check that an answer is correct. "The warranty is 15 years" overlaps well with a document that says "12-year warranty". Catching that needs a model that reads the document and the answer together: an LLM judge.

    ▶ Lab: Pro bonus, LLM-as-a-judge (Colab Pro only)

    On an L4 or A100 runtime, a 7B model grades each pair from 1 to 5 for faithfulness, and you keep only pairs scored 4 or 5. On the free T4 the notebook skips this cell. Module 2 spends a whole section on making judges like this trustworthy.

    The notebook saves the Q&A set as sft_qa.jsonl.

    Wrap-up

    Key takeaways

    • Look at the data first. Every rule in this module came from a problem visible in the raw table.
    • Cheap steps first, expensive steps last. Rules and hashing remove most of the junk for almost no compute; LLMs only see what survives.
    • Every filter is a trade-off. Too strict and you lose good data; too loose and junk gets through. The checks in this lab test both sides.
    • Synthetic data needs checking. Generating pairs is easy; making sure they're faithful to the source is the real work.

    At production scale: NVIDIA NeMo Curator

    The lab uses small open-source libraries so it runs on a free GPU. NeMo Curator runs the same steps on GPUs and across many machines, for terabytes of data:

    Lab NeMo Curator
    clean_text UnicodeReformatter, custom DocumentModifier
    Word-count and repetition rules WordCountFilter, RepeatingTopNGramsFilter and other filters via ScoreFilter
    langdetect FastTextLangId
    MD5 / MinHash ExactDuplicates / FuzzyDuplicates, plus semantic deduplication
    Presidio PiiModifier
    Q&A generation and filtering Synthetic data pipelines, quality classifiers, reward-model filtering

    Common pitfalls

    • Writing filters without looking at the data first
    • Removing <script> tags but leaving the code inside them
    • Removing only exact copies, so edited copies and HTML mirrors survive
    • Never tuning the deduplication threshold
    • Redacting every entity type, so error codes and numbers disappear
    • Trusting synthetic data without checking it against the source
    • Not recording what each step removed, so you can't explain or reproduce the dataset

    Glossary

    Term Meaning
    Boilerplate Repeated non-content text: menus, footers, cookie notices
    NFKC A unicode normalisation form that maps equivalent characters to one standard form
    Shingle A run of k consecutive words, used to represent a document as a set
    Jaccard similarity Shared items divided by all distinct items across two sets
    MinHash A compact signature whose match rate estimates Jaccard similarity
    LSH A bucketing scheme that finds likely-similar pairs without comparing every pair
    PII Personally identifiable information
    NER Named-entity recognition: a model that tags names, places and organisations
    Grounding Whether a generated answer is supported by its source document
    Model collapse Quality loss from repeatedly training on model-generated data
